# Googleyness / Leadership

A distinct behavioural component in the reported loop 📄, prepared for separately from the technical rounds.

---

## The themes named 📄

| Theme | Named |
|---|---|
| Leading | Leadership, Mentoring, Influencing without authority |
| Friction | Conflict, Technical disagreements, Difficult decisions |
| Things going wrong | Failure, Projects that didn't go according to plan, Handling mistakes |
| Operating | Ambiguous problems, Ownership, Prioritization |

Twelve themes, but they collapse into about five real stories 🧩 — one project that went wrong, one disagreement you resolved, one thing you owned end to end, one ambiguous problem you scoped yourself, one person you grew. A good story answers three or four of the themes depending on which angle the follow-up takes.

## The structural advice 📄

> "I used the STAR structure as a starting point, but I tried not to memorize scripts. The better approach, in my opinion, is to know your stories deeply enough that you can adapt them based on the follow-up questions. **Because there WILL be follow-ups.**"

And separately, listed under what went well:

> "For behavioral and architecture questions, real examples from my work were much stronger than hypothetical examples."

## Why scripts fail here specifically 🧩

A memorised STAR answer is optimised for a single telling. The failure mode is precise: you deliver a polished 90 seconds, the interviewer asks *"what would you do differently?"* or *"what did the other person think was happening?"* or *"how did you know that was the right call?"* — and the script has no answer, because the script was a performance rather than a memory.

Knowing the story *deeply* means being able to answer from angles you didn't rehearse:

- What did the other side actually believe, and were they partly right?
- What did it cost — in time, trust, or scope?
- What did you get wrong, and when did you realise?
- What would you do differently, concretely?
- Who disagreed with the retrospective?

**A note on the failure stories** 🧩: "failure", "projects that didn't go to plan" and "handling mistakes" are three of the twelve themes. That's a quarter of the list about things going wrong, and it's where candidates most often sanitise. A failure story with no real cost and a tidy redemption reads as evasion. The credible version names the actual damage, your specific contribution to it, and what changed in how you work — without theatrical self-flagellation either.

**On "influencing without authority"** 🧩 — this is the one most specific to senior levels, and the weakest answers describe *persuading by being right*. The stronger version shows you understood what the other team was optimising for, and found a framing where your proposal served their goal too. Being right is table stakes; the question is about the part after that.

## The gap only you can close 🧩

Nothing in this repo can supply your stories. The concrete work:

- [ ] Five stories, written down, each with the measurable outcome
- [ ] For each, four follow-up angles answered — cost, the other side's view, what you'd change, what you got wrong
- [ ] At least two where the outcome was genuinely bad
- [ ] Said out loud, timed — the post's *"practice communication"* point applies here as much as to the technical rounds

## Team fit / team matching 📄

Worth separating from the behavioural round, because it's a different stage with a different question:

> "At this stage, the focus shifts from *'Can this person clear the technical bar?'* towards *'Is there a team where this person's experience and interests are a good match?'*"

The candidate was still in this stage when writing, in conversations about which teams fit their ML/GenAI and large-scale production background. Two implications 🧩: clearing the loop is not an offer, and your answer to *"what kind of ML problems do you want to work on"* — asked as early as the recruiter screen — is load-bearing here, not small talk.
